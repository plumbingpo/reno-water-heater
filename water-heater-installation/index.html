Not every water heater job is a straight replacement. Plenty of Reno, NV projects start with a finished basement or a new addition that needs its own heater. Other jobs involve moving a unit out of a cramped closet, stepping up to a bigger tank for a growing family, or switching fuels entirely. All of them mean new gas piping, vent routes, circuits, or water lines, planned out before anyone cuts a pipe. We take on installations of every size, from a small point-of-use heater under a sink to a whole-house tankless system.

Before any pipe is cut, we size the heater to your household. For tanks, first-hour rating counts more than gallons; for tankless, it is gallons per minute at your winter temperature rise. Next, we choose the spot together. A gas model has to vent outdoors and draw enough combustion air. A heat pump model has to have breathing room, temperatures above roughly 40 degrees, and a condensate drain. Electric tankless models often require a dedicated high-amperage circuit. Anywhere a leak could ruin finished space, a drain pan goes underneath. You get clear choices and a firm price before work begins.

Installation day runs on a checklist: gas lines sized, run, and leak-tested with a sediment trap at the heater, vents built to the manufacturer's size and pitch, circuits and breakers matched to the heater's load, and water lines connected with shutoffs and dielectric protection between copper and steel. A full-size relief valve discharge line runs to a safe point, and any required expansion tank, drain pan, seismic straps, or permit is part of the job. To finish, we fill and purge the tank, test draft and carbon monoxide on gas units, set the thermostat near 120 degrees, and walk you through the new setup. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Big-tub or multi-shower demand the current heater cannot meet
  • A property where the water heater has to go in from scratch
  • A tank that cannot keep up with your household anymore
  • Missing code items like an expansion tank, drain pan, or proper venting
  • Taps at the far end of the house that take minutes to warm up
  • A water heater installed above finished rooms with no drain pan
  • An addition too far from the existing heater for quick hot water
  • A planned switch from gas to electric, or to a heat pump model

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Start with the gas line. An undersized line starves a new heater whenever the furnace or range runs, and a whole-house tankless unit usually needs more gas than the existing line carries. Vent material, size, pitch, and clearances all have to be right, or exhaust drifts back inside. Electric models need their own math on breaker, wire gauge, and panel capacity. Add relief valve piping, dielectric fittings, expansion tanks, drain pans, seismic straps, and permit rules that vary from one jurisdiction to the next, and there are a lot of details to get right.

A professional installation covers the whole job, not just setting the heater. We size by first-hour rating or flow rate, run and test the gas piping, route the vent to spec, match the circuit to the load, and add expansion tanks, drain pans, or straps where required. Before we go, gas units are checked for draft and carbon monoxide. The result is an installation that is safe, sized correctly, and ready for inspection. If a smaller or simpler heater fits your needs better, we will tell you directly.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Tankless heaters need a yearly flush to clear scale from the heat exchanger
  • Keep flammable liquids and clutter away from gas heaters
  • Check the drain pan and relief valve discharge line now and then for signs of water
  • Do not box the heater in; leave room for service and airflow
  • Set the thermostat near 120 degrees for safety and efficiency
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years to protect the tank
  • Drain a few gallons from a new tank twice a year to control sediment
